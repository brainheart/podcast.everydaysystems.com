Everyday Systems Podcast — Episode 108: Digital Methadone

In this episode: Screen addict? Take a page from opioid treatment and find your digital methadone.

Download mp3

Hi, this is Reinhard from Everyday Systems.

I’ve spoken before about how I take a break from all but certain whitelisted technology every weekend between breakfast and dinner, “weekend luddite.” It’s a system that is both increasingly challenging to practice as technology becomes more pervasive, and increasingly necessary. My compliance isn’t perfect, and I keep having to diddle with what exactly is on the whitelist of approved technological activities, but I’m so glad I’m making this struggle and reducing, at least a little, the time I spend staring at screens.

Today I’m going to tell you about another method I have for reclaiming screen time – more in terms of quality than quantity, though I think it’s also indirectly reduced my total screen time. I call it Digital Methadone, which is a term I now see has been used 172 times already according to google, so I can’t claim complete originality in terms of the name, but it describes the method too perfectly for me to fish around for another one, so I’m going to stick with it.

The idea is this: there are many situations in which we automatically reach for our phones and start diddling. We are bored for half a second, waiting in line, in the bathroom, whatever, and before we know it the phone is out and we are on social media, or doomscrolling newsfeeds, or paralyzing our brains with video shorts. You know what I’m talking about, I don’t have to get into a long description.

Now it would be great if we didn’t whip out the phone immediately in such situations all the time and if you can pull that off, God bless you. You have figured out something even better than the system I am about to describe.

But I am working under the assumption that that level of restraint is more or less impossible for most people, at least given their current level of digital addiction. We need that digital dopamine hit, and everywhere we go, we see everyone else doing it too, it’s like the whole world is this giant digital meth den with everyone constantly shooting up all around us. It’s a tough place to get sober.

So: let’s do what they do to treat actual opioid addiction. Replace the problematic, destructive apps with something innocuous or even good. Or at least, less bad. Get rid of the worst apps on your phone – social media, video shorts, newsfeeds. The digital heroin. Then add a replacement quick fix app (or apps) that doesn’t play into your schadenfreude, envy, or outrage. Something where you learn something. Something that doesn’t get you all riled up or turn your brain off completely. And resolve to make that app the first thing you open every time you compulsively, aimlessly pick up your phone. This is your digital methadone.

What makes a good methadone app? Five things.

  1. It should have low start up costs. You should be able to fire it up immediately and be absorbed.
  2. It should also be easy to put down again. There should never be a “wait I’m in the middle of something I can’t interrupt” feeling.
  3. You should actually kind of enjoy it. Otherwise there’s no way it’s going to replace the tempting bad stuff.
  4. It should have some redeeming value – like, you are learning something. Not just turning off your brain
  5. It should not be getting you all riled up and stoking negative emotions. You should not feel gross or unclean or angry after using it.

It’s surprisingly difficult to find an app that meets all these criteria. In my case, the app that does it is Anki. A-N-K-I. It comes from a Japanese word meaning memorization or learning by heart. It’s this open source, community built flash card creation and review app that (among other things) is beloved by jeopardy champions and medical students who have to retain vast amounts of information.

I’ve spoken about anki before, in my study habit episodes, so I’m not going to get too deep into the specifics of it again today. It’s an incredible learning tool, but this episode I just want to focus on this tremendous side-effect benefit it has of serving as screen time mitigation, why it is such good digital methadone.

(Incidentally, I love it when Everyday Systems feed into each other and support each other like this, when a system intended to fix one kind of problem turns out to also be a big help with a seemingly totally unrelated one. This is one big reason why I’m able to practice so many systems. They can actually start to make the others easier once you’ve piled on a few and you get these virtuous circles going.)

The one thing I will say about anki cards as anki cards, specifically, is that if the idea of flash cards doesn’t immediately appeal to you, because you think, “what do I need to memorize? I’m not in school anymore (THANK GOD!) or learning a language or going on jeopardy any time soon,” I’ve found that I use anki for a wide variety of non-academic or fact accumulation reasons. Like, I read something in a book or article on any subject, that is so funny or striking or wow and I want to periodically call it to mind. I’ll copy the passage into an anki card and blank out one or two especially striking words. Then when I review the card, I have to fill in those words. That quick little fill in the blank exercise calls the whole passage back into mind, and to an extent the whole larger work or context it was taken from. It keeps the whole thing from fading completely. I even have snippets of emails in there that made a big impact on me, to remind me periodically of those people and circumstances. So it’s really much broader than just an academic study tool. It’s good for anything on which you want to periodically reflect. And I think most of us probably do have something like that in our lives. So give it a second look even if the idea of flashcards doesn’t immediately appeal to you.

It’s super quick to review an anki card. I don’t have to type anything, I just guess in my head, and then press a button indicating whether I got it right or wrong. It trusts me not to bullshit myself and that makes review very quick and efficient. Plus it’s a sign of respect, that trust. I’m not tempted to game the system – I am the system. I can also stop any time without losing anything. There’s no session or lesson I have to finish. It’s just one card at a time. So it checks those first two Digital Methadone boxes big time.

It’s also fun. It has redeeming value. You don’t feel gross using it. You are using it to stay close to whatever you have decided interests you or is important to you or that you love. It doesn’t infuriate you because of that respect I mentioned of it trusting you to judge whether you got the answer right, instead of the antagonistic relationship apps like Duolingo push you into where the app decides and then you get annoyed and want to defeat it. Like when it mis- transcribes a word and I don’t get credit when I should. Or when it dings me for not translating something into the occasionally awkward English it expects. It can feel antagonistic and I find myself wanting to game the system, and occasionally succeeding in doing so, which is idiotic considering that my goal is to learn something. Also, sometimes Duolingo is hard to interrupt, like if you’re in the middle of the lesson and you don’t want to lose your progress, and you often have to talk to it, which can be awkward in certain situations. There’s something about the casino dings and flashes that makes it feel a little unsettlingly TOO much like the real thing, the real digital heroin.

My goal today is not to sell you on anki specifically. My goal is to sell you on finding YOUR digital methadone, the app that keeps you from resorting to the apps you wish you used less, that you reach for unthinkingly and compulsively and feel bad about using afterwards. I’m just going through it as an example that checks all the boxes and how. Another potential example that I don’t have personal experience with is readwise, an app that sort of automatically does some of what I use anki for; it takes sections you’ve highlighted in your ebooks and plays them back for you to review. Very similar to one use of anki that I described, and if you’ve been highlighting already for a while, it sounds like an elegant way to leverage that work you’ve already done.

But it’s hard for me to recommend specific other apps because I don’t have one. I don’t need one. Anki does it all for me. It fills this role completely. When I look at my iphone app usage, Anki is consistently, by far, my most used app of any on my phone. I was actually surprised to see how much this is the case when I looked just now for this episode. And delighted when I saw no social media, no news, no games, no video shorts.

So what about the digital heroin apps? What do you do with them? I mentioned before, that I try to get them off my phone completely. That’s not always possible. And sometimes they have some redeeming value when used in a non-compulsive way. So I try to install these on my computer, not my phone, and use them only in tightly limited ways.

News, for example, does have some redeeming value, or it can at least. But I don’t think the way most of us are consuming it these days most of the time does. We motive mixologists know that all motives are mixed, right? The reasons we need our news hit are complicated. There’s some impartial civic concern lurking in there, maybe. But there’s also: entertain me, enrage me, let me feel that delightful at first but then not so delightful rush of hatred for the political enemy. Group hatred for the other group, which I think we feel more than enough of already.

So by all means, read the news. Even participate in social media. But not impulsively on your phone. Get these apps off your phone entirely, if possible. Then set aside a dedicated, limited time to check them on your computer. Or do what I do and don’t set hard limits but just track the time you spend on these activities, so you can see how much time you are spending on them, so it doesn’t sneak in under the radar. This can be really simple, like keeping a log of when you are reading news and social media, with start time and stop time. You can do this very simply in a google doc or spreadsheet. Then, like an over-full plate on the No S diet, it gets embarrassing if it’s too much, when you force yourself to see that too much. And that gentle pressure will nudge you to prioritize what is actually good in those activities, and in any case limit the bad. I’ve spoken about this technique before, of logging news and social media time: proactive procrastination. It pairs very well with digital methadone. It may even be necessary for it.

That’s it, that’s Digital Methadone. I hope you find it helpful or at least thought provoking. It’s made a big difference in my life by focusing on the quality of my screentime, and then also indirectly reducing the quantity.

Thanks for listening.
