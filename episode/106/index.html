Everyday Systems: Podcast : Episode 106

Everyday Systems Podcast — Episode 106: Narralogue

In this episode: Make language learning a habit, not a goal. No app, no book, no $$$, just 10 minutes a day of talking to yourself.

Hi, this is Reinhard from Everyday Systems.

Do you remember the Study Habit, my family of systems for making study a lifelong habit instead of sporadic cramming? I haven’t talked about it in a while, but I’ve been practicing it assiduously, and it’s probably, in terms of the time I actually devote to it, the branch of Everyday Systems that occupies me most. Also, possibly, the branch of Everyday Systems I enjoy the most. Today I’m going to talk about a particular study habit system I’ve developed for learning languages and how I’ve been applying the study habit mentality to language learning more broadly.

I call my system Narralogue. It’s a portmanteau of narrate and monologue. In the best Everyday Systems tradition, the core idea is very simple: you talk to yourself out loud in a foreign language for a set amount of time every day. You set a timer for some relatively small amount of time (I’m currently doing 10 minutes) and then describe (or narrate) what it is you see around you in the language you are learning.

That’s it. Mechanically, that is the whole system. Amazing, right? But maybe you don’t see immediately why it is so powerful. Let me explain.

1. Sophisticated and Substantial Language Production

You talk to yourself in order to exercise the language production pathways that get neglected by more passive approaches. The way to learn a language is to speak it, and this is a low-friction way of doing that (except you look crazy if you do it on the street walking your cat as I do – I guess some people might consider that friction). You just set a timer, describe what you see/hear/sense around you, and strive to speak without pausing until the timer goes off. The goal is to speak as many words as possible in a natural conversational cadence for the set amount of time. You don’t ever want to allow more than a second or two between any two words. Ideally you will speak in complete, grammatical sentences, but if you're not that far advanced, sentence fragments or even baby talk single words will do. If you don’t know how to say something, try to stay in the language and circumlocute around it. Get as close as you can, even if it’s a little ridiculous. These “forced circumlocutions” may be the most valuable part of Narralogue, what will really train you to be able to hold conversations with other humans in the target language. Because inevitably you will find yourself in situations like this, where you are out of your depth, and you still need to be able to make yourself understood.

Other approaches may have an element of active word production, canned conversations with Duolingo AI, fill-in-the-blank testing with flash cards, but they are limited and artificial. With Narralogue you are producing hundreds of words a session and are challenged to use them in realistic and sophisticated ways by reality itself.

2. Focus on the physical world

You are allowed to discuss things that are not physically in front of you, there are no hard rules about subject matter. The only hard rules are the time and the continuous flow of words. But like meditation coming back to the breath, it's a good thing to return to your present sensations and perceptions when you catch yourself wandering. The rationale is there is always something in front of you to describe so you never have to scratch your head to think what to say. You are never at a loss for subject matter, like you might be with more abstract or remote topics. With your subject matter right out there in front of you, you should have no trouble speaking without pause until the timer goes off, producing as many words as possible.

Another reason that focusing on the immediate physical world is so valuable is that it is the most important foundation for language and having to describe it brings you back to that. Even metaphorical and abstract speech is founded on the physical world and movement within it, that’s what the metaphors and abstractions refer to. And these basic building blocks change a lot from language to language and are key to pinning a language down. They’re basic, but they’re also surprisingly variable from language to language. Like, Spanish has two verbs for “to be.” German declines nouns differently depending on whether they’re involved in movement towards a destination or just sitting there. So when you see a squirrel passively sitting on a branch and then suddenly jumping to another tree, and you’re describing it all, you have to exercise that distinctly German mental machinery. And all languages have so many different prepositions to express spatial relationships that don’t quite overlap 100% with similar terms in other languages. A different language isn’t just a different 1:1 substitution cipher for the world. It organizes the world differently. It’s got its own unique “physics.” Coming back to the physical world again and again forces you to confront that fundamentally different physics at the heart of each language and grapple with it until it really clicks.

This isn’t just something that’s good to practice, in terms of being able to produce grammatical sentences in that language, it’s mind expanding to be aware of. How can languages disagree on the importance of these fundamental distinctions about reality? And yet they do. And yet they all still work. It’s a little shocking to think how constrained by language some of our most basic intuitions are — and how effectively, but still, imperfectly, we compensate. It’s one thing to know this intellectually, it’s another thing to experience it, repeatedly, on a daily basis.

Because I start to associate my Narralogues with these physical things around me, sometimes later in the day when I walk past them, I’ll automatically start describing them in my head in one of my target languages. Sometimes I’ll even catch myself mumbling out loud. This is another example of the unplanned “eureka time” I described in my previous study habit episodes. It’s bonus practice that you get for free from having invested the dedicated up-front time, and a great sign that the habit is really taking. It’s like you ignite something with your dedicated practice and then it starts to burn on its own.

3. Speak out loud and bold

Usually I do my Narralogue very early in the morning when there aren’t many other people around. It’s just me and my cat, and he doesn’t mind me talking to myself. But if someone comes close enough to overhear me, or maybe it’s someone I know and should say hi to, I’ll pause the timer so as not to be antisocial or too crazy seeming. Sometimes I’ll switch to whispering or even silent mental speech if the person isn’t too close but truthfully that looks even more crazy than talking out loud sometimes. But as soon as I can I switch back to talking out loud. I don’t know if it exercises different mental machinery than silent thought does, maybe, but the main reason I prefer it is it’s all too easy when I’m not talking out loud to forget what I am doing and wander out of the target language. The talking out loud keeps me on target. Whisper mode and subvocalized thought mode are good to have in your pocket for certain situations, but out loud mode should be the default. Increasingly, when other people are around and I don’t want to seem too crazy, I lean toward phone mode, where I press my phone against my face and pretend I’m talking to someone on it.

Talking out loud, boldly, helps you practice and develop courage, a specific kind of courage. You get used to hearing your voice in the other language. You get used to hearing yourself make mistakes and speaking with an imperfect accent and sounding stupid and not dissolving. It’s like a kind of solo exposure therapy. Then maybe you’ll be less afraid to make mistakes and speak with actual other people in the language. This is a big hurdle for me, particularly with German, the language I know best, besides English. I am embarrassed, having the most German name ever, that my German is not better. For a normal American, even an American of German parentage, my German is pretty good. But for a Reinhard, my German is terrible. I must have the worst German of any Reinhard on the planet. Narralogue helps me narrow the gap a little between me and the next worst Reinhard, but more importantly, it helps me accept and not be ashamed of it.

4. Maintenance vs. Progress

Narralogue is in my "Study Habit" family of systems, which treats study as an ongoing habit like diet or exercise vs. some temporary and goal oriented thing, and it ties in very nicely with some of my other study habits as well. It’s less about quick progress than “can you do it forever?” Like the No S Diet or Shovelglove, it’s “maintenance first.” The idea is not that you're going to learn a language to some degree of proficiency and just stop, but that you’ll want to keep it going lifelong. That seems obvious when I say it like that, and yet, I’ve never heard it articulated as a concern of any other language learning methodology. Take immersion for example. The gold standard. Clearly effective. Great idea if you can swing it — but this is not an easy thing to pull off for most people at most stages of life. And even if you can immerse for a while in a foreign country, what do you do when you un-immerse again, to keep it up? Unless you’re emigrating, immersion is intrinsically temporary.

And then, if you do emigrate, you’ve gotta worry about not losing your native language. I know from my parents that this is actually possible, to a degree. They were both German immigrants to the U.S. My mom’s job involved her speaking German every day, and she had a lot of German-speaking friends, but my dad’s work was not like this, and he was not very sociable, so by the end of his life, he basically didn’t speak any language fluently.

It’s strange and a little disturbing that this is possible, but I don’t think it’s that unusual with immigrants. And there are even more extreme cases that I have heard of. In 1940, a 20-year-old French soldier (he want by several name for various incomprehensible reasons, but one of them was Paul Catrain) was captured by the Germans and sent to a POW camp. At the end of the war, he was liberated by the Soviets, but not allowed to return home to France until the Soviet Union collapsed in the 1990s. That whole time he’d been living in a village in the Ukraine, without anyone to speak French to, and by the time he made it back to France, he could no longer hold a conversation in his native language.

Narralogue is easy to keep going because you never run out of material. If one corner of the physical world no longer challenges you walk a couple blocks to another. If mornings get old, switch to nights. The physical world is a big and varied place. Though I suspect even in one familiar spot there is an infinity to notice and describe.

5. The Study Habit Ecosystem

Narralogue doesn’t require any tech or even materials at all (except a timer). It’s very much another “app for mentats.” You could practice it on a desert island, just you and the palm tree. But it does benefit from being practiced in coordination with other systems and it can be interesting to track and assess your progress using technology.

Narralogue isn’t going to be the only system you use to learn a language. You’re going to have to use other tools and systems as well. But it fills an important gap and it plays really nicely with these other tools and systems. Almost all other tools and apps and methods are big on input, low on output. Even when they have output, it’s kind of dumb output, fill in the blank output, finish the script output, say exactly what’s expected output. They’re imbalanced. Narralogue gives you lots of output — sophisticated, individualized, creative output — at very low cost in terms of effort or overhead or certainly money.

So how does Narralogue fit into the other systems and tools I am using?

As I’ve mentioned before, I do Duolingo. It’s far from perfect, and sometimes it enrages me (it doubles as anger management training for me not to curse at the thing). But in conjunction with everything else I’m doing, I find it helpful. But what’s really great is the feedback loops between systems, how one practice can enhance the other. This isn’t so much the case with Duolingo, but it is with some of my other language learning practices. For example, ChatGPT voice mode, which I try to have a daily conversation with in my target language. When I notice I have trouble describing something in Narralogue, and I’ve been perplexed by it often enough to remember to do some follow-up homework on it, I’ll ask about it when I’m doing my daily ChatGPT voice mode session. I’ll ask for example sentences. What gender the word is. How it differs from similar-sounding words or whether it maps cleanly to a single English word. Then I might take snippets of the auto-generated transcript of that conversation and use it as the basis for creating custom Anki cloze-deletion flash cards. This is where you just blank out bits of text and prompt yourself to fill them in. It’s a fantastic technique for quickly generating high-quality, targeted flash cards. The cloze-deletion technique also lets you stay 100% within the target language, you don’t need an English prompt or translation, the context is clue enough. And then, next Narralogue session, when the word or concept comes up again, I can actually apply it. It comes full circle. Voice mode helps my Narralogue because I follow up on difficulties I run into there, and Narralogue helps my voice mode because I always have some difficulty to get the conversation started. I’m never like, “uh, chat, what should we talk about?”

Yes, AI can be a crutch. Cognitive surrender and all that. But you don’t have to use it that way. It can be the opposite. Push it, challenge it, keep digging deeper. Your crutch is now a Shovelglove. “Right relationship with robots” not “no relationship” — that is the Everyday Systems way.

Once a week for each of my target languages I’ll also record my 10-minute Narralogue session for analysis by AI. I vibecoded a little script that analyzes these recordings and spits out a variety of stats about how many words I’m producing, what CEFR level it thinks I’m currently on, mistakes it notices coming up over and over again, and so on. I’m hoping I’ll be able to see real, measurable improvement soon, but it’s early days still. Just 7 months or so since I started doing Narralogue of any kind, and barely 2 since I’ve been doing these recordings.

I also listen to podcasts and audiobooks – “AV Didact.” My German is good enough that I can listen to normal German podcasts and news and audiobooks. Audible has a very good selection. I watch German YouTube and Netflix and other streaming video. I try to only watch or listen to news in German – this doubles as a good way to keep my compulsive total news consumption in check. And it’s not unusual that I’ll hear something in one of these AV Didact sessions that that Narralogue has sensitized me to and that I’m now able to eagerly assimilate. It’s exciting when this happens! And it happens a lot.

I incorporate the language of the day into many of my other routines; every day I have a language that I focus on. I write my Bracket journal entry by hand in that language, record short Audiodidact output memos, and listen to translated versions of my daily Mantrafication. Basically, any routine task I can do in the language of the day, I try to do in that language. It’s a good excuse for more practice without taking any additional time, and exercises slightly different pathways.

6. Mindfulness

When you add a new routine to your life, a new daily task to check off, it can feel stressful. More rule burden, more task burden. We’ve talked about that a lot recently. But Narralogue can feel really nice, like I’m taking a step back from the scramble. It’s got a definite mindfulness component. You are describing the world around you, the world in front of your eyes, and so you have to pay attention to it, maybe in a way that you haven’t ever really before. This is a nice side benefit, for a language learning system. Maybe even nicer than the main benefit.

When I’m doing Narralogue, I’m almost always with my cat, Misha, wandering around right outside our house at the same time every morning. We usually see the same old things every day – except I’m continually astonished at what I haven’t ever really noticed before. Or some little alley, like 10 feet from my house that he drags me to that I’ve physically never even set foot in before. It’s astonishing to realize how set in our ways we are, physically and mentally. Narralogue can help jar you out of that. Or at least be appropriately disturbed by it.

7. Prioritization through suffering

You might find yourself repeating things a lot. Especially if you do Narralogue in the same physical environment most days, as I do, walking my cat at 6:15 in the morning in front of my house. On the one hand, you might worry that this becomes monotonous and unchallenging. But I suspect it is going to take a while for that to happen, that you’ll have to get pretty good first. That might be the ultimate sign that this is really working, a problem to strive for. In the meantime, the repetitiveness is good — especially when you are feeling unsure and inadequate about what you are repeating, when you worry that what you are repeating are mistakes. It helps you learn. It helps you prioritize. It reminds you of your stubborn difficulties day after day after day until you overcome them. It helps you feel productively stupid about still not knowing how to say x and eventually nudges you to figure it out. It makes you wonder why Spanish and German have two words for “car” (for different reasons!) and why you might want to use one or the other. You start to associate vocabulary with your specific external environment and the physical world becomes a mnemonic device, a kind of organically grown memory palace.

Sometimes I can think of several ways to say something in the language, and I’m not sure which is best. If three expressions come into my mind, I’ll say all three, and see which sounds best, which tastes best, which has the best mouthfeel. Sometimes I’m not sure. But over time, I start developing an ear for it, a taste for it, at least a sensitivity towards it so my ears perk up if I hear one or other of the formulations again. If the doubts linger and it keeps coming up, eventually the question will stick beyond the Narralogue session and I’ll look it up online, or discuss with ChatGPT voice mode. If I notice I’ve looked it up or talked it through with AI more than once, I’ll make an Anki cloze-deletion card, as I described before.

I don’t use my phone to look things up or talk to AI during a Narralogue session. Because then I’d be waiting and listening most of the time. Narralogue is about speaking, about production. Not because these other things aren’t also valuable, but because language production is so hard to squeeze in. It’s so painful that we avoid it at all costs. I can do the looking up and listening later. Now it is time to produce. I do sometimes record a question to follow up on later in my voice recorder. Or take a picture. The important thing during Narralogue, is that I keep the words coming out of my mouth. And the gap helps with prioritization. If I forget to follow up on something once, that means it wasn’t so important for me to learn right now. But if I forget and remember a few times, and start remembering that I’ve forgotten, that’s an increasing signal that maybe it is important. The process of forgetting and remembering not only helps me filter, but will sear it into my brain like it never would have been otherwise. It uses this apparent weakness as a strength.

Mistakes are painful. And you’ll notice yourself making them a lot with Narralogue. That’s good. You have to embrace that pain. The pain of mistakes is self-knowledge. You now know what you don’t know. You are learning it. You are learning what questions to follow up on, what you are unsure about. Maybe you forget right away. But keep doing Narralogue and you will catch yourself noticing the same mistakes over and over again. There’s that same house across the street. There’s that same telephone pole and the same guy walking the three-legged dog you saw yesterday. There’s the same thought I had about these things last time and the same doubt about whether I articulated it correctly. The little repeated cuts in the same spots will go deeper and deeper. You will forget and forget and remember and remember and eventually the important mistakes will go deep enough that you’ll remember to follow up on them. And you’ll follow up on the right things, the important things, because the pain has prioritized them for you. And after it happens you’ll actually remember what that follow-up was because of that beautiful, stubborn knot of neural pathways the pain has built for you.

There’s even something beyond knowledge, something of spiritual value in confronting your own stubborn stupidity, something not to be gotten over, something to be embraced. As Simone Weil wrote in Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God:

“Above all it is thus that we can acquire the virtue of humility, and that is a far more precious treasure than all academic progress. From this point of view it is perhaps even more useful to contemplate our stupidity than our sin. Consciousness of sin gives us the feeling that we are evil, and a kind of pride sometimes finds a place in it. When we force ourselves to fix the gaze, not only of our eyes but of our souls, upon a school exercise in which we have failed through sheer stupidity, a sense of our mediocrity is borne in upon us with irresistible evidence. No knowledge is more to be desired.”

There you have it — Narralogue: a language learning technique, a mindfulness practice and a course of continuing education in humility. Also a great way for me to spend quality time with my cat. The languages I am currently using this technique on are German (5 sessions a week) and French (2 sessions a week). And I’m starting to squeeze in Spanish too (2 sessions a week). I’ve added in a couple of PM sessions as well, on my walk home from work. My German was pretty good already when I started Narralogue, French I had some experience with, and Spanish I just started recently to keep my son company now that he’s taking it in middle school. It’s too soon to claim any miraculous improvements, and it’s also confounded by all these other things I’m doing at the same time, but Narralogue seems to be helping with all three of these languages at these very different levels of maturity for me. I’ll report back in 2027 to let you know how it’s holding up.

That’s all for today – thanks for listening.
